<p>Some novels open with a death. <cite>The Salt Orchard</cite> opens with an inventory. Nell Garrow, a fruit scientist who has spent twenty years cataloguing heritage apples for a seed bank, arrives at her late aunt&rsquo;s orchard on the northern coast and counts what is left: forty-one trees, a cider press with a cracked screw, two elderly goats, and a leather-bound ledger in which every harvest since 1911 has been written down in the same small, slanting hand.</p>
<p>It&rsquo;s the second book in Mira Hollis&rsquo;s Tidewater sequence, after <a href="{{< relref "/reviews/the-tide-ledger" >}}"><cite>The Tide Ledger</cite></a>, but you could start here. What connects the books is the coast itself (the same estuary, the same salt wind that strips the bark from the seaward side of every tree) rather than any plot you&rsquo;d need to remember.</p>

<h2>A ledger that seems to know</h2>
<p>The hook, and it is a good one, is that the ledger&rsquo;s oldest entries seem to anticipate the orchard&rsquo;s worst years. A line from 1953 reads like a description of last spring&rsquo;s blight. Hollis never lets this tip into the supernatural. Instead, Nell does what a scientist does: she cross-references, she charts, she argues with herself in the margins. The mystery of the ledger becomes a way for her to avoid the mystery of her aunt, a woman she loved and never really knew.</p>
<p>What I admired most is how patient the book is. Chapters are named for apple varieties, and each one moves through a single season of work: pruning, grafting, the long anxious weeks of blossom. If that sounds slow, it is, deliberately. By the time the autumn storm arrives in the final third, you know every tree on that slope, and you feel each loss.</p>

<h2>The people on the slope</h2>
<p>Hollis is very good on work, and on the people who do it. Danny Aske, the neighbour who has quietly kept the trees alive for three winters without being asked, could have been a sentimental figure. Instead he is prickly, proud and funny, and his friendship with Nell is the warmest thing in the book.</p>
<p>There&rsquo;s also Ruth, the aunt&rsquo;s former partner, whose letters run through the second half and who refuses, to the very end, to tell Nell what she wants to hear.</p>

<h2>Where it stumbles</h2>
<p>The ledger thread resolves a little too neatly. A late revelation about who wrote the 1953 entries ties things up like a puzzle box, while the rest of the novel is happy to leave things open. Readers who want a strong plot engine may also find the middle stretch, the summer chapters especially, becalmed.</p>
<p>I didn&rsquo;t mind. I read the last eighty pages in one sitting on a wet Sunday, then went back to reread the opening inventory, which means something quite different once you know what&rsquo;s coming.</p>

<h3>Who it&rsquo;s for</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Readers who love slow, place-rooted novels about work and family</li>
  <li>Anyone who has sorted through a relative&rsquo;s belongings and found a stranger there</li>
  <li>Probably not for readers who need a twist every chapter</li>
</ul>
